Behind the analysis

How this was built

The project behind the dashboard: where the data comes from, what it took to make it trustworthy, and the decisions that shaped every number on the page.

Dashboard · Findings report

Start with a question, not a dataset

San Francisco publishes every restaurant health inspection. Most projects on this data stop at a map of scores. The more useful business question is about the system: when the city flags or closes a food business, does the business actually fix the problem, and does the fix last? That framing turns a public dataset into an evaluation of whether enforcement works, with findings someone could act on.

The short version of what it found: San Francisco responds to failures within days, and most fixes stick. But one in five does not last a year, and 150 failures have no recorded follow-up.

Getting the data, and keeping it fresh

The core data comes from San Francisco's open data portal through its API: every health inspection from 2024 to today, refreshed weekly by an automated pipeline. Two supporting sources answer questions the inspection data alone cannot: the city's business registry (is a business still alive?) and the Yelp API (do customer ratings know anything about kitchen hygiene?). Neither shares an ID with the health department, so linking them required name-and-address matching, which the analysis labels as inferred rather than measured wherever it appears.

Making the data trustworthy

This is where most of the analytical effort went. Raw open data is never analysis-ready, and every finding on the dashboard sits on top of problems that had to be found, measured, and handled:

  • The same inspection appears multiple times. The city republishes corrected records. The pipeline keeps every version and always analyzes the latest one, so corrections improve the data instead of double-counting it.
  • Recent records are incomplete on purpose. The inspection-type label is filled in by the city with roughly a year's lag; all of the newest year has no labels yet. Analyses were designed around the rating field, which does not lag.
  • Not every "facility" is a facility. One farmers-market permit produced 19 same-day inspection rows, one per stall. Treating those as one facility's timeline would be nonsense, so umbrella permits are identified and excluded from facility-level analysis.
  • ~110 permit types, from restaurants to tattoo parlors. Health inspections cover massage parlors, laundromats, and tobacco shops too. Every permit type was reviewed and classified, with an automated test that fails if an unknown type ever appears.
  • One row did not belong. A single inspection dated 2031, after its own publish date. Found by checking, excluded with a comment.

The discipline behind all of this: every excluded row is accounted for by name. The staged data reconciles exactly against the raw data, and 26 automated tests re-verify the assumptions on every weekly refresh.

Making the numbers honest

The easy version of this analysis would be wrong in quiet ways. The methods were chosen to avoid that:

  • Silence is not success. A failed facility with no later inspection has not "stayed fixed", it just has not been observed. Durability is only measured where a follow-up actually exists.
  • Compare on the same clock. Closed facilities get re-checked within weeks; conditional passes within months. Comparing their "still passing" rates naively looks fair and is not. The analysis uses a fixed one-year window for both.
  • Small samples do not get quoted. Any group under a minimum sample size shows its count instead of a rate, on the dashboard and in the report.
  • Cross-check before concluding. 150 failures were never re-inspected. Business-registry and Yelp data triangulate them: most are businesses that are demonstrably still open, which turns "missing data" into the project's sharpest finding, an enforcement follow-up gap.
  • Name the confounds. Neighborhood failure rates track business mix (restaurant-heavy districts fail more). Yelp comparisons carry a coverage bias (enforcement lands on restaurants; the comparison group includes markets Yelp does not list). Both caveats travel with the numbers wherever they appear.

Building the site

The dashboard is deliberately simple technology doing careful work. Charts are hand-built SVG following a validated accessibility method: the color palette is machine-checked for color-blind safety in both light and dark themes, every chart has a data-table twin for screen readers and skeptics, tooltips add detail but never hide it, and every rate on the page carries its sample size. All filtering happens in the browser against pre-computed data, which is why the page needs no server and updates itself when the weekly pipeline redeploys it. Each chart also states its takeaway in plain text, so the reading is never left to chart interpretation alone.

Who this is for

For diners and residents, the dashboard is a practical tool: look up any restaurant before you eat there and see its real inspection history, not its star rating. For food-safety awareness, it makes a public record legible that normally sits in a database nobody reads. And for the city and anyone who holds it accountable, it measures the enforcement system itself: San Francisco responds to failures within days and most fixes stick, but one in five does not last a year, and a set of failures with no recorded follow-up deserves attention. Public data should be able to answer whether public systems work; this project is that idea, executed on one system.
