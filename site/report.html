Do health inspections work in the city?

San Francisco food-facility health inspections, January 2024 to July 2026. Interactive dashboard · Methodology

When San Francisco's health department flags a food facility with a Conditional Pass, or closes it outright, three things can happen next: the facility fixes the problem, the fix holds, or the whole episode repeats. This analysis follows every failure in the public inspection record through that sequence: 20,673 inspection events at 7,777 facilities, including 1,126 failures at 846 of them.

The short answer

Enforcement responds fast and mostly works on the first pass, but one in five fixes does not survive the year, and the record contains a set of failures with no documented follow-up at all.

Response: days, not months

After a closure, the median facility is re-rated the next day (n=452); after a Conditional Pass, within 8 days (n=674). 87% of all failures have an observed re-rating. Whatever else is true of this system, it does not sit on closures.

Outcome: the first fix mostly works

At re-rating, 75.1% of closed facilities pass (293 of 390 observed); 89.2% of conditional passes clear (523 of 586). Mean violations drop from 7.3 to 4.6 after closures, 7.4 to 3.6 after conditional passes. The harsher the sanction, the harder it is to clear on the first attempt.

Durability: one in five fixes fails within the year

Among episodes that resolved to a Pass and had a full observable year after resolution, 20.2% of closure episodes (34 of 168) and 20.6% of conditional-pass episodes (52 of 252) failed again within 365 days.

Measured at a fixed horizon deliberately. The naive version ("still passing at next observation") makes the two groups look identical at 86-87%, but their observation clocks differ: post-closure facilities are re-checked at a median of 41 days, post-conditional at 207. Comparing those percentages is comparing different clocks.

Context: after an ordinary Pass, facilities fail their next observation 4.5% of the time (n=11,474). Post-enforcement facilities fail theirs at roughly three times that rate. The data cannot distinguish "enforcement failed to reform them" from "enforcement selects facilities that were always higher-risk"; both readings are consistent with what is measured.

The repeat core

24.9% of all failures are a facility's second or later. 189 facilities (22% of those that ever failed) failed at least twice; 57 failed three or more times. A small core of facilities generates a quarter of the enforcement load.

The failures nobody followed up

150 facilities have a failure with no later rated visit in the record. Cross-referencing two independent sources splits them:

Some registry lag is expected, but the triangulation makes "quiet deaths" an unlikely explanation for most of them. On this record, they read as enforcement follow-up gaps.

What do facilities fail for?

Parsing every violation out of the inspection record answers a natural question first: when a facility fails, what did the inspector actually find? Rarely one thing. A conditional pass logs 7.6 violations on average and a closure 8.2, and the typical failure spans 4.3 distinct problem categories at once.

Across the 1,126 failures since 2024: cleaning and sanitizing problems appear in 71%, vermin (cockroaches, rodents, flies) in 69%, temperature control in 59%, equipment or facility disrepair in 55%, handwashing and employee hygiene in 49%, cross-contamination in 44%, plumbing and water in 34%, and food storage in 33%. And vermin is the signature of the serious cases: 81% of the 452 closures cite an infestation. Sanitation and temperature problems put a facility on a conditional pass; vermin is what most often shuts one down.

A failure counts toward a category once, no matter how many violations of that kind it logged, so the shares sum to more than 100%. Legal boilerplate segments (closure notices, document requests) are excluded. Categories come from the parsed violation text; see the method note below.

Does the violation predict the relapse?

Mostly no, and that is informative. Parsing the inspection record's violation text into categories (temperature control, vermin, handwashing, contamination, equipment, and so on) lets us ask whether some kinds of problems come back more than others. They barely do: relapse within a year runs 15-20% for every major category, against a 20.5% baseline (pooled across both failure types), and having more high-risk violations at the failure does not raise it either. Facility and equipment issues sit at the low end (15-16%) and vermin and temperature problems at the high end (about 20%), a gradient too small to lean on at these sample sizes.

The sharper reading: relapse is a property of the facility, not of the violation. Which fits the repeat-offender finding above, where 22% of ever-failed facilities produce a quarter of all failures. If the goal is preventing repeat failures, targeting facilities with a failure history beats targeting any particular violation type.

Method note: each violation was extracted from a concatenated text field (a parser validated against the recorded violation counts handles 97%; an AI model re-parsed the remainder, with every row checked against the inspector's recorded count) and classified by a reviewed keyword ruleset. As a robustness check, an AI model independently re-classified every violation description: the two methods label individual descriptions differently often enough (65% agreement), yet the finding does not move. Under either classification, relapse stays in a narrow flat band across all categories, which is exactly what a facility-level (not violation-level) effect predicts. Groups under 30 episodes are not quoted.

Do Yelp ratings know any of this?

Essentially no. Facilities that were ever closed average 3.85 stars (n=306); facilities never flagged average 3.99 (n=568). Statistically, the relationship between a facility's rating and how often it failed is essentially zero (r = -0.065 on a scale where 0 means no relationship; n=1,296). Consumer ratings and kitchen hygiene are close to independent signals, which replicates published findings in other cities.

Read with the coverage gap in mind: 86% of enforcement-history facilities matched on Yelp versus 67% of the comparison group, because enforcement lands mostly on restaurants while the comparison group includes markets and cafeterias Yelp does not list. And the ratings are a snapshot from today measured against inspections from the past two years, so this shows association only, not cause and effect.

Do public complaints catch what inspections miss?

Anyone can report a food facility to the health department, and the department does respond: the record since 2024 shows 808 complaint-triggered visits (677 first visits, 131 follow-ups) plus 268 foodborne-illness investigations, reaching 721 distinct facilities. What those visits find is the surprise. About nine in ten log zero violations (91% of first complaint visits and of illness investigations, against 21% of routine inspections), and only 2.7% of rated complaint visits end in a failure, against 9.0% of routine inspections. Of the 452 closures on record, 16 came from a complaint visit and 1 from an illness investigation; routine inspections and their re-inspections produced 345.

The blunt reading: scheduled surveillance, not the public, does the catching. That is not an argument against the complaint line, which is how the city hears about problems between visits. But as a detection instrument it runs far behind the routine calendar, and it pairs with the Yelp result above: neither the public's reviews nor its complaints reliably see what an inspector sees.

Two caveats. A complaint visit is scoped to the allegation, so zero violations means the specific complaint was not substantiated on arrival, not that the kitchen passed a full checklist. And the city publishes neither complaint dates nor content, so how fast a complaint becomes a visit cannot be measured here. Of the 452 closures, 79 carry no inspection-type label yet (type labels in this dataset lag about a year), so the per-type closure counts are floors, not exact splits.

The geography of enforcement

Does the system behave differently by place? Raw failure-rate maps cannot answer that: facility mix varies by district (restaurant share of rated inspections ranges from 41% in District 10 to 77% in District 1, and tracks the failure rate), so a district's rate partly measures what kinds of businesses it zones for. The system's own behavior is the cleaner question, and it splits three ways:

  • Closure response is uniform. Median 1 to 3 days to re-rating in every district. The sharpest sanction is geography-blind.
  • Conditional-pass follow-up varies. Median 7-8 days in Districts 1, 3, 4, and 7, versus 11 (District 2), 14 (District 11), and 19 (District 10); the slower cells are small (n = 14-51), so read as a pattern to watch, not a verdict.
  • Follow-up gaps cluster. The share of failures older than 90 days with no re-rating ranges from 4-8% (Districts 1, 7, 4, and 3) to 21-26% (Districts 10, 11, and 2), a sixfold spread. Notably this does not map onto a simple income gradient: District 2 (Marina) sits alongside Districts 10 and 11 (the southeast) at the top.

Per-district failure counts run 29 to 296, so medians are quoted and small cells flagged. No demographic data is joined: overlaying income or race on this n would imply causal claims the data cannot support. What is defensible: follow-up consistency varies by place far more than response speed does, and the districts where failures go unresolved deserve the first audit.

The verdict

As an emergency response, yes. San Francisco's system reacts to failures within days, not months, and most facilities fix the problem on the first try. The sharpest sanction, closure, is applied uniformly across the city. That part of the system works, and the data shows it clearly.

As a cure, only partly. One in five fixes does not survive the year, and a facility that has failed once is about three times more likely than average to fail again. What a facility failed for barely matters; which facility it is matters a great deal. A small core, 22% of ever-failed facilities, generates a quarter of all failures.

And it has a blind spot. Around 120 facilities failed an inspection, stayed open by every available record, and were never re-inspected. That follow-up gap is the single clearest finding a health department could act on from this analysis.

Three practical takeaways. For diners: a restaurant's star rating tells you nothing about its kitchen; the inspection record, which this site lets you search, is the only signal there is. For the city: the enforcement playbook works at the moment of crisis, and the highest-value improvements are following up on the facilities that slipped through and watching the small group of repeat offenders rather than any particular violation type. For anyone who cares about public data: every finding here came from records the city already publishes. The answers were sitting in the open; the questions just needed to be asked.

Recommendations

These are the actions an outside analyst can responsibly suggest from public data alone. Each one traces to a finding above, and each is measurable, so the city could tell whether it worked.

1. Work the follow-up queue

Around 120 facilities failed an inspection, remained open, and were never re-inspected. A simple aging report, every failure with no re-inspection after 90 days, would surface them immediately. The metric to watch: percent of failures re-rated within 90 days, which currently sits at 81.5% and should be near 100%.

2. Schedule by facility history, not violation type

Relapse barely varies by what a facility failed for, but concentrates heavily in facilities that failed before: 22% of ever-failed facilities generate a quarter of all failures. Re-inspection frequency weighted by a facility's failure history, for example a shorter routine cycle after a second failure, would put inspector time where the risk actually is.

3. Add a durability check

One in five fixes fails within a year, and the current pattern is to return quickly after the failure, then not again until the normal cycle. A single check roughly six months after a resolved failure would catch backsliding while it is cheap to correct.

4. Watch follow-up consistency across districts

Time to re-inspection after a conditional pass ranges from about a week in some districts to nearly three in others, and the share of failures left unresolved varies sixfold. The counts per district are small, so this is a pattern to monitor rather than a verdict, but it is exactly the kind of operational metric a dashboard like this one could track continuously.

5. Publish better data

Some gaps in this analysis are publishing choices, not analytical ones: inspection-type labels arrive up to a year late, violations are published as concatenated text rather than structured records, and umbrella permits are not flagged. Fixing any of these would make the public record more usable for everyone, including the department itself.

What this data cannot establish

  • Causality. No counterfactual exists for what facilities would have done without enforcement.
  • Silence is not success. A failure with no later inspection has an unknown outcome, not a good one (statisticians call this censoring). Whether a fix lasted is only measured where a follow-up inspection actually exists.
  • Recent inspection types are incomplete. DPH backfills the type label with roughly a year's lag (100% of 2026 rows lack it); every metric here uses ratings, which do not lag.
  • Cross-source linkage is inferred. The business registry and Yelp have no shared key with DPH; matching is by name and address.

Method, briefly

The data flows automatically each week from the city's open-data API into a pipeline that keeps every version of every record and always analyzes the most recent one (the city republishes corrected records). Roughly 110 permit types were reviewed to isolate food facilities; farmers-market permits, where one permit can carry 19 same-day stall inspections, are handled separately; and every excluded record is accounted for by name, with the cleaned data reconciling exactly against the raw feed. Facility histories and enforcement episodes are built in SQL on top of that, guarded by 26 automated tests that re-check the assumptions on every refresh. The whole pipeline runs in the cloud on a weekly schedule, and this page regenerates with it.
