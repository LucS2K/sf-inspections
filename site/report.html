The findings · January 2024 - July 2026

Do health inspections work in the city?

When San Francisco’s health department flags a food facility with a Conditional Pass, or closes it outright, three things can happen next: the facility fixes the problem, the fix holds, or the whole episode repeats.

This analysis follows every failure in the public inspection record through that sequence: 20,673 inspection events at 7,777 facilities, including 1,126 failures at 846 of them.

The short answer

Enforcement responds fast and mostly works on the first pass, but one in five fixes does not survive the year, and the record contains a set of failures with no documented follow-up at all.

The first fix: fast and mostly works

After a closure, the median facility is re-rated the next day (n=452); after a Conditional Pass, within 8 days (n=674), and 87% of all failures have an observed re-rating. The outcomes mostly justify the speed: 75.1% of closed facilities pass at re-rating (293 of 390 observed), 89.2% of conditional passes clear (523 of 586), and mean violations drop from 7.3 to 4.6 after closures and 7.4 to 3.6 after conditional passes. The harsher the sanction, the harder it is to clear on the first attempt. Whatever else is true of this system, it does not sit on failures.

But one in five fixes fails, and it’s the same facilities

Among episodes that resolved to a Pass and had a full observable year after resolution, 20.2% of closure episodes (34 of 168) and 20.6% of conditional-pass episodes (52 of 252) failed again within 365 days. For context, after an ordinary Pass, facilities fail their next observation 4.5% of the time (n=11,474); post-enforcement facilities fail theirs at roughly three times that rate.

And the relapses concentrate. 24.9% of all failures are a facility’s second or later: 189 facilities (22% of those that ever failed) failed at least twice, and 57 failed three or more times, so a small core of facilities generates a quarter of the enforcement load. The data cannot distinguish “enforcement failed to reform them” from “enforcement selects facilities that were always higher-risk”; both readings are consistent with what is measured.

Measurement note: why a fixed one-year window

Durability is measured at a fixed horizon deliberately. The naive version (“still passing at next observation”) makes the two groups look identical at 86-87%, but their observation clocks differ: post-closure facilities are re-checked at a median of 41 days, post-conditional at 207. Comparing those percentages is comparing different clocks. More in the methodology.

The failures nobody followed up

The ~120 are the uncomfortable finding: businesses the registry and Yelp both say are alive and operating, whose failing inspection was never followed by another recorded visit of any kind. Some registry lag is expected, but the triangulation makes “quiet deaths” an unlikely explanation for most of them. On this record, they read as enforcement follow-up gaps.

What facilities fail for (and why it barely matters)

The conclusion first: relapse is a property of the facility, not of the violation. Relapse within a year runs 15-20% for every major violation category, against a 20.5% pooled baseline, and having more high-risk violations at the failure does not raise it either. If the goal is preventing repeat failures, targeting facilities with a failure history beats targeting any particular violation type.

As for what inspectors actually find: failures are pile-ons, not single issues. A conditional pass logs 7.6 violations on average, a closure 8.2, and the typical failure spans 4.3 distinct problem categories, led by cleaning and sanitizing (in 71% of the 1,126 failures), vermin (69%), and temperature control (59%), ranging down to food storage (33%). Vermin is the signature of the serious cases: 81% of the 452 closures cite an infestation.

Categories come from the parsed violation text, and a failure counts toward a category once, so shares sum past 100%. Full parsing and classification detail, including the robustness check, is in the methodology.

The public doesn’t see it

Yelp first. Facilities that were ever closed average 3.85 stars (n=306); facilities never flagged average 3.99 (n=568), and the relationship between a facility’s rating and how often it failed is essentially zero (r = -0.065; n=1,296). For diners the implication is direct: a restaurant’s star rating tells you nothing about its kitchen, and the inspection record, which this site lets you search, is the only signal there is.

Complaints do little better as a detection tool. Since 2024 the city logged 808 complaint-triggered visits plus 268 foodborne-illness investigations, yet about nine in ten log zero violations (against 21% of routine inspections), and of the 452 closures on record, 16 came from a complaint visit and 1 from an illness investigation. Scheduled surveillance, not the public, does the catching. The complaint line still matters as the city’s ear between visits; it is just not how problems get found.

Reading notes: coverage and scope

The Yelp comparison carries a coverage gap: 86% of enforcement-history facilities matched on Yelp versus 67% of the comparison group, because enforcement lands mostly on restaurants while the comparison group includes markets and cafeterias Yelp does not list. Ratings are also a present-day snapshot against two years of inspections, so this shows association, not cause and effect.

On complaints: a complaint visit is scoped to the allegation, so zero violations means the complaint was not substantiated on arrival, not that the kitchen passed a full checklist; the city publishes neither complaint dates nor content, so complaint-to-visit speed cannot be measured; and 79 of the 452 closures carry no inspection-type label yet (labels lag about a year), so per-type closure counts are floors, not exact splits.

The geography of enforcement

Does the system behave differently by place? Raw failure-rate maps cannot answer that: facility mix varies by district (restaurant share of rated inspections ranges from 41% in District 10 to 77% in District 1, and tracks the failure rate), so a district’s rate partly measures what kinds of businesses it zones for. The system’s own behavior is the cleaner question, and it splits three ways:

  • Closure response is uniform. Median 1 to 3 days to re-rating in every district. The sharpest sanction is geography-blind.
  • Conditional-pass follow-up varies. Median 7-8 days in Districts 1, 3, 4, and 7, versus 11 (District 2), 14 (District 11), and 19 (District 10); the slower cells are small (n = 14-51), so read as a pattern to watch, not a verdict.
  • Follow-up gaps cluster. The share of failures older than 90 days with no re-rating ranges from 4-8% (Districts 1, 7, 4, and 3) to 21-26% (Districts 10, 11, and 2), a sixfold spread. Notably this does not map onto a simple income gradient: District 2 (Marina) sits alongside Districts 10 and 11 (the southeast) at the top.
Reading these district numbers

Per-district failure counts run 29 to 296, so medians are quoted and small cells flagged (how small samples are handled). No demographic data is joined: overlaying income or race on this n would imply causal claims the data cannot support. What is defensible: follow-up consistency varies by place far more than response speed does, and the districts where failures go unresolved deserve the first audit.

The verdict

As an emergency response, yes. San Francisco’s system reacts to failures within days, not months, and most facilities fix the problem on the first try. The sharpest sanction, closure, is applied uniformly across the city. That part of the system works, and the data shows it clearly.

As a cure, only partly. One in five fixes does not survive the year, and a facility that has failed once is about three times more likely than average to fail again. What a facility failed for barely matters; which facility it is matters a great deal. A small core, 22% of ever-failed facilities, generates a quarter of all failures.

And it has a blind spot. Around 120 facilities failed an inspection, stayed open by every available record, and were never re-inspected. That follow-up gap is the single clearest finding a health department could act on from this analysis.

Recommendations

Each action traces to a finding above and is measurable, so the city could tell whether it worked.

1. Work the follow-up queue

Around 120 facilities failed an inspection, stayed open, and were never re-inspected; a simple aging report (every failure with no re-inspection after 90 days) would surface them immediately. Metric to watch: percent of failures re-rated within 90 days, currently 81.5%, target near 100%.

2. Schedule by facility history, not violation type

Relapse barely varies by what a facility failed for but concentrates in facilities that failed before, so weight re-inspection frequency by failure history (for example, a shorter routine cycle after a second failure). Metric to watch: share of failures that are repeats, currently 24.9%.

3. Add a durability check

The current pattern is to return quickly after a failure, then not again until the normal cycle; a single check roughly six months after a resolved failure would catch backsliding while it is cheap to correct. Metric to watch: the one-year relapse rate, currently about 20%.

4. Watch follow-up consistency across districts

Time to re-inspection after a conditional pass ranges from about a week in some districts to nearly three in others; the counts are small, so monitor it as an operational metric rather than a verdict. Metric to watch: per-district share of failures unresolved after 90 days, currently spanning 4-26%.

5. Publish better data

Some gaps in this analysis are publishing choices: inspection-type labels arrive up to a year late, violations ship as concatenated text rather than structured records, and umbrella permits are not flagged. Metric to watch: the lag between an inspection appearing and its type label arriving.

What this data cannot establish

The biggest limit is censoring: a failure with no later inspection has an unknown outcome, not a good one, so silence is never counted as success. The other limits (no causal counterfactual, lagging type labels, inferred cross-source matching) are catalogued in the methodology.
